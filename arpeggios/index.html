REFERENCE / ARPEGGIOS

A chord, one note
at a time.

Arpeggios turn a strummed block of sound into a moving line. They are how guitarists play gentle intros and verses, fingerstyle accompaniment, and solos that follow the chords. This page covers what they are, where they are on the neck, and how songs use them.

01 / WHAT IS AN ARPEGGIO?

Same notes, spread out in time.

Strum a C chord and C, E and G sound together. Play them one after another and you have a C arpeggio. Nothing about the harmony changes, only the rhythm and texture.

Chord

All notes at once. Full, rhythmic, loud.

Arpeggio

The same notes one by one. Clear, flowing, lets each note ring.

Scale

Every note of the key, stepwise. Arpeggios skip the notes between chord tones.

Two ways to use them

Accompaniment: hold a chord shape and pick its strings in a pattern. Lead: play the chord's notes as a melodic line, often in a single-note shape.

The formula is the chord

Major arpeggio = 1 3 5, minor = 1 b3 5, dominant 7 = 1 3 5 b7. Know the chord formula and you know the arpeggio.

Why it sounds "right"

Every note is a chord tone, so over the matching chord nothing clashes. That makes arpeggios the safest notes in any solo.

How to learn a shape
  • Play it low to high and back, one note per click, saying the degree out loud (1, 3, 5…).
  • Then play the matching chord and arpeggio back to back; hear that they are the same sound.
  • Move the shape to a new root: it's movable, like a barre chord.

05 / SOLOING WITH ARPEGGIOS

Make the solo follow the chords.

In a lead, play each chord's arpeggio while that chord sounds. Stay in one area of the neck and start each new arpeggio on the note nearest to where the last one ended. The line moves smoothly and the harmony is clear even without a band.

  1. 1

    Outline, then decorate

    Start with pure arpeggios like above. Once they're comfortable, add one scale note between chord tones as a passing note. That's how most melodic solos are built.

  2. 2

    Land on the 3rd

    The 3rd of each chord says "major" or "minor" most clearly. Aim for it on beat 1 when the chord changes.

  3. 3

    Use 7th arpeggios for jazz and soul

    Four-note arpeggios (1 3 5 7) over 7th chords sound sophisticated immediately. In a ii–V–I, the 7th of one chord often moves down a half step to the 3rd of the next.

  4. 4

    Superimpose for colour

    Over Am, an arpeggio of C (C E G) gives Am7's 3rd, 5th and 7th. Playing the arpeggio a 3rd above the root adds extensions without new shapes.

06 / PRACTICE

Exercises and common mistakes.

Ten minutes a day on these turns arpeggios from a shape into a sound you reach for without thinking.

Daily exercises

  • Chord → arpeggio: strum a chord once, then pick its notes. Repeat through G, C, D, Em.
  • One pattern, many chords: keep the rolling pattern going while changing chords every bar. Don't stop the right hand.
  • Metronome ladder: 60 BPM eighth notes; go up 4 BPM only after three clean loops.
  • Shape in 3 places: play the C major arpeggio with the root on string 6, 5 and 4.
  • Nearest-note changes: loop Am–F–C–G, starting each arpeggio on the closest note to the last.

Common mistakes

  • Lifting fingers too early. Hold the full chord so notes ring into each other.
  • Bass note on the wrong string. The bass should usually be the chord's root; check which string it's on for each chord.
  • Uneven timing. Arpeggios expose rhythm; count out loud.
  • Too loud a bass. Balance the thumb with the fingers.
  • Only going up. Practise descending and zig-zag orders too.
