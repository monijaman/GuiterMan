PRACTICE / MUSIC NOTATION

Music Notation

Learn to read guitar music through staff diagrams, counted rhythms, rests and worked melodies. Every score uses standard guitar octave notation.

01 / LEARN & PLAY

Read height as pitch.

The five lines and four spaces form a staff. A treble clef tells you which pitches they represent. Read from left to right; higher notes on the page are higher in pitch. Lines, bottom to top: E4, G4, B4, D5, F5. Spaces: F4, A4, C5, E5. Octave numbers distinguish one C from another.

Ledger lines extend the staff. Middle C (C4) sits on the short line below it; D4 is in the space just below the staff.

02 / LEARN & PLAY

Read the shape as duration.

In 4/4, a quarter note lasts one beat. A filled head with a stem is a quarter; add a flag for an eighth. A hollow head with a stem is a half; a hollow head without a stem is a whole. Notes may have stems pointing up or down; their duration stays the same.

Whole

4 beats: count 1–2–3–4.

Half

2 beats: count 1–2.

Quarter

1 beat: one per click.

Eighth

½ beat: count 1 & 2 &.

Tempo tells you the speed. ♩ = 60 means sixty quarter-note beats per minute. Clap the rhythm before trying to fret the notes.

03 / LEARN & PLAY

Silence and held notes count too.

A rest is measured silence. Keep counting while you do not play. A dot adds half of the original value: a dotted half lasts 3 beats; a dotted quarter lasts 1½ beats. A tie joins two notes of the same pitch into one held sound. A slur joins different pitches and usually asks for connected playing.

04 / LEARN & PLAY

Check time, key and accidentals first.

The top number of a time signature tells you how many units are in a bar; the bottom number tells you the note value of that unit. In 3/4, count three quarter-note beats. In 6/8, there are six eighth notes, usually grouped into two pulses: ONE two three FOUR five six.

A sharp (♯) raises a note one semitone; a flat (♭) lowers it one semitone; a natural (♮) cancels a sharp or flat. On guitar, one semitone is one fret. An accidental normally applies to the same pitch and octave for the rest of that bar; a barline resets it to the key signature.

A key signature places sharps or flats after the clef and applies them in every octave. G major has F♯; F major has B♭. C major and A natural minor have none. Chord symbols above the staff describe the backing harmony, not extra notes you must play in the lead.

05 / LEARN & PLAY

Connect the staff to your guitar.

Guitar treble notation is conventionally written one octave higher than it sounds. These pages follow that convention: written E5 is the open high e string, which sounds E4. Standard tuning, from low to high, sounds E2–A2–D3–G3–B3–E4.

Tab gives a playing location: the top line is high e; numbers are frets; 0 means an open string. Standard notation gives pitch and rhythm. The same pitch can appear on more than one string, so tab provides one suggested fingering.

Expression marks: p = soft, f = loud, crescendo = gradually louder. A dot above a note means staccato (short), unlike a duration dot beside the note. Start with clear timing before adding slides, bends or vibrato.

06 / LEARN & PLAY

Your first two-bar reading exercise.

  1. Check the clef and 4/4 time. There are no key-signature sharps or flats.
  2. Name the pitches slowly, then clap four quarter notes followed by a half and two quarters.
  3. Play at 60 BPM; hold E for two clicks in the second bar.
  4. Repeat while looking at the staff, using tab only when you need the location.
