REFERENCE / MUSIC THEORY

Chords are the places.
Scales are the roads.

Music theory in pictures, for guitar. Start with the smallest step between two notes, build the major and minor scales, number the chords in any key, then use it all to link chords with walking bass notes so your playing moves instead of jumping.

START HERE / THE BIG DISTINCTION

Scale or chord progression?

They work together, but they answer different questions. A scale chooses the notes; a chord progression chooses the order of the chords.

SCALE = THE NOTE POOL

Which notes can I use?

A scale is a row of notes played one at a time. Use it for melodies, solos, fills and bass walks.

Which chords come next?

A progression is a sequence of chords played one after another. Use it for the harmony and structure of a song.

How they connect on guitar: the C major scale supplies the notes; stacking every other note builds C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am and Bdim; choosing an order such as C → G → Am → F creates the progression. Think: notes → chords → order.

01 / HALF STEPS & WHOLE STEPS

Twelve notes, then they repeat.

All of Western music uses just 12 notes. The distance from one to the next is a semitone (half step, S). Two semitones make a tone (whole step, T). Every scale is a pattern of T and S.

Seven letters

A B C D E F G, then A again. These are the "natural" notes: the white keys.

Two gaps are missing

There is a sharp/flat between every pair of letters except B–C and E–F. Those pairs are only a semitone apart. This one fact explains every scale shape.

Sharps and flats

The note between A and B is A# (A raised) or B♭ (B lowered): same sound, two names. Use whichever keeps each letter once in the scale.

02 / THE MAJOR SCALE

T – T – S – T – T – T – S

Start on any note and take these seven steps and you get that note's major scale. From C it uses only white keys: C D E F G A B C. The semitones fall on 3→4 and 7→8.

Change the key: the pattern stays the same and only the note names change.

Root Scale note Bass zone: strings E, A, D, frets 0–5, where most chord links happen

03 / THE NATURAL MINOR SCALE

T – S – T – T – S – T – T

The minor scale moves the semitones: 2→3 and 5→6. A minor uses the same notes as C major (its relative major), starting from A: A B C D E F G A.

Why this matters for linking chords

Walks between major chords often step in whole tones. Walks that start on a minor chord often go tone then semitone, because in the minor scale the half step comes straight after the root. You don't need to memorise this: follow the scale and the steps come out right.

04 / THE CHORD NUMBERING SYSTEM

Every key has the same seven chords.

Instead of learning chord names key by key, learn one numbered pattern that works in all twelve keys. This section builds it up in small steps, from a single chord to every key at once.

4A

Why bother with chord numbers?

Why it matters: without numbers, every key is a new list of chord names to memorise. With numbers you learn one pattern and use it everywhere.

Understand songs fast

A friend says "it's a 1–4–5 in A". With numbers you instantly know: chords 1, 4 and 5 of A major = A, D and E. No chord sheet needed.

Change key in seconds

Song too low for your voice? G – C – D is I – IV – V in G. Move it to A and it's simply A – D – E. Same song, new key.

Link chords smoothly

Knowing the key tells you which scale your bass walks use (sections 05–08). The numbers tell you the key.

Remember: a number (I, IV, V…) is a chord's position in the key. The name changes from key to key; the number doesn't.

4B

One chord: the 1–3–5 rule

Why it matters: before you can number the chords of a key, you need to know how a single chord is built. It is always the same recipe.

A basic chord (a triad) is just three different notes: the 1st, 3rd and 5th notes of the scale, counted up from the note the chord is named after. That is the whole rule. For C: count C (1) D (2) E (3) F (4) G (5), keep 1, 3 and 5, and you get C–E–G, the C chord. Every chord on this page, and in the table below, is built this way.

1 = the root

The note the chord is named after, and usually the lowest note you play. It tells your ear "this is home for this chord". In a C chord the 1 is C.

3 = the colour

The 3rd decides major or minor. 4 frets above the 1 = major (happy, bright). 3 frets above the 1 = minor (sad, soft), written ♭3. Change only this note and C becomes Cm.

5 = the support

The 5th is 7 frets above the 1 in both major and minor chords. It makes the chord sound full and solid. Power chords (C5) are just 1 and 5 with no 3, which is why they are neither major nor minor.

Quick trick: skip every other letter

The note letters go A B C D E F G and round again. A triad uses every other letter: C–E–G, D–F–A, E–G–B, F–A–C, G–B–D, A–C–E, B–D–F. Sharps and flats change with the key (D major is D–F#–A), but the letters always skip like this.

Why skip notes? Why not 1–2–3?

Notes next to each other in the scale (like C and D) rub against each other when played together. Notes two steps apart (a 3rd) blend smoothly, so stacking 3rds gives a chord that sounds settled. That's why the rule is "take one, skip one".

Major is 1–3–5, minor is 1–♭3–5. Do I need to work out which?

Not inside a key. If you count 1–3–5 using only the key's own scale notes, the right 3rd comes out automatically: from C you get E (major), from D you get F (minor). That is exactly why every key ends up with the same pattern of major and minor chords, as the four steps below show.

What comes after 1–3–5?

Keep skipping and you get the 7th: 1–3–5–7 makes seventh chords like Cmaj7 (C E G B) or G7 (G B D F). Sus chords swap the 3 for a 2 (sus2) or a 4 (sus4). They are all the same counting idea.

Remember: a chord = notes 1, 3 and 5 counted up the scale from the chord's own name.

4C

The seven chords of a key

Why it matters: apply the 1–3–5 rule to every note of a scale and you get exactly the chords that sound "right" together in that key.

  1. 1

    Write out the key's scale

    A key is a home note plus its scale. The key of C uses the C major scale: C D E F G A B. Those seven notes are the only notes the key "allows".

  2. 2

    Build a chord on each note

    Use the 1–3–5 rule on every scale note: start on it, skip one, take one, skip one, take one. From C: C (skip D) E (skip F) G = C–E–G, the C chord. From D: D F A = Dm. Do this for all seven notes.

  3. 3

    Some come out major, some minor

    The scale mixes tones and semitones, so the gaps inside each chord differ. Bottom gap of 4 frets then 3 = major. Bottom gap of 3 frets then 4 = minor. 3 then 3 = diminished (rare, you can mostly skip it).

  4. 4

    Number them 1 to 7

    Give each chord the number of the scale note it starts on, in Roman numerals: I II III IV V VI VII. Write major ones in CAPITALS and minor ones in small letters: I ii iii IV V vi vii°.

4D

Same pattern in every key

Why it matters: this is what the title means. Learn the pattern once and you know the chords of all twelve major keys.

It does not mean every key uses the same chord names. C major and G major use different chords. It means every major key has the same pattern of seven chords: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished. Learn that one pattern and you know the chords of all twelve major keys.

4E

Move a song to any key

Why it matters: this is the payoff. Write a song as numbers and you can play it in whatever key suits your voice, your capo or the singer.

Read down any column: the names change, but the type never does. The IV chord is always major, the vi chord is always minor. So a song written as I – V – vi – IV is C–G–Am–F in the key of C, G–D–Em–C in the key of G, and D–A–Bm–G in the key of D. Same song, just moved to a key that suits your voice or your capo.

WHY NUMBERS BEAT NAMES

Learn where a chord sits in the key once, and you never have to relearn it. The vi chord is always the minor chord three frets below the home note: Am in C, Em in G, Bm in D.

Guitar makes it even easier: the root notes of I, IV and V form a fixed hand shape, shown below.

Remember: to change key, keep the numbers and look up the new names. On guitar you can often just slide the same shape.

4G

Good to know

Why it matters: these answer the questions most people ask next. Open the ones you care about.

Which chords matter most for a beginner?

I, IV and V (the three major chords) carry thousands of songs on their own. Add vi and you can play most pop songs. ii and iii show up less often, and vii° is rare in strummed guitar music.

How do I find the key of a song?

Usually the key is the chord the song starts and ends on, the one that sounds like "home". If a song uses G, C, D and Em, it is almost certainly in G: those are G's I, IV, V and vi.

What about minor keys?

Minor keys use the same trick with the minor scale, which gives the pattern i, ii°, III, iv, v, VI, VII. A minor has exactly the same chords as C major (Am Bdim C Dm Em F G), just counted from A instead of C. Switch the MODE menu in 4F to "Minor keys" to see them all and hear them.

What if a song uses a chord that isn't in the table?

That happens, and it's fine: songwriters borrow chords from outside the key for colour (a common one is a major III, like E in the key of C). The table shows the chords that fit the key naturally, which covers most of what you'll play.

Example (key of G)

G major is G A B C D E F#. So the IV chord is C major, the vi chord is E minor and the ii chord is A minor.

Relative keys share chords

A minor's chords (Am Bdim C Dm Em F G) are C major's chords starting from vi. The vi of a major key is the i of its relative minor.

Numbers make walks easy

I → V, IV → I, vi → IV… once you know the key, you know which scale to walk: the key's scale. Every chord in the table is built from it.

Remember: I, IV and V are the most useful chords to learn first in any key. Add vi and you can play most pop songs.

05 / BASS NOTES

Every chord stands on its root.

The lowest note you strum is the bass note. In open chords it is normally the root, the note that names the chord. Linking chords means joining these bass notes, so you need to know exactly where each one sits.

06 / THE LINKING METHOD

Join the dots with scale notes.

A bass walk is a tiny melody in the low strings that leads the ear from one chord's root to the next. You play one or two notes of the scale between the chords, so the change sounds planned instead of abrupt.

  1. 1

    Find both bass notes

    Where does the chord you're on start, and where does the next one start? C → G means C (A string, 3rd fret) to G (low E, 3rd fret).

  2. 2

    List the scale notes between

    Use the key's scale. Between C and G going down in C major: B, A. Those are your linking notes, usually two of them.

  3. 3

    Pick the shorter direction

    Down from C to G is 2 notes; up is 4 (D E F G). Take the short road, which usually means the lower G the chord uses anyway.

  4. 4

    Play in time, land on beat 1

    Strum the first chord, play the linking notes on the last beats of the bar, and hit the new chord exactly on beat 1. The walk leads there; don't be late.

08 / CHROMATIC LINKS

When the scale has nothing in between.

Some chords are too close together: G to A is a single tone and the scale has no note between them. Then you walk chromatically, fret by fret, even through a note that isn't in the key. It works because the passing note is short and leads straight to a strong note.

Rule of thumb: chromatic notes go on weak beats and never stay. A passing B♭ lasting one beat sounds bluesy and intentional; the same note held for a bar sounds like a mistake.

09 / RHYTHM & TIMING

Count it, then walk it.

The notes are the easy part. The skill is keeping time: the strumming and the walk share one bar, and the new chord must arrive on beat 1 of the next bar. Here are four ways to fit a walk into a bar of 4/4.

Practise it apart first

Many students find it hard to keep the strum going while fretting single notes. Loop just the walk notes with a metronome, then add the chords.

Pick, don't strum

Play the linking notes with a single down-stroke each (or alternate down–up for eighth notes). Aim the pick at one string only.

Swing or straight?

If the song swings, the walk swings too. Practise a swing feel on muted strings before combining it with links.

ONE WEEK · 10 MINUTES A DAY

Practice plan

  1. Day 1 · Say the notes of C major and G major out loud while playing them on the A and low E strings.
  2. Day 2 · C → G and G → C only. Slow, with a metronome at 60–66 BPM.
  3. Day 3 · Add A → D and D → A. Notice C# (a note outside C major) now belongs.
  4. Day 4 · Minor walks: Am → C, Em → G. Feel the tone–semitone.
  5. Day 5 · Chromatic links: G → Am, C → D, D → E.
  6. Day 6 · Play section 10 (G–Em–C–D) all the way through without stopping.
  7. Day 7 · Pick a song you know. Add one link per verse, at the most important chord change.
!

WATCH FOR THESE

Common mistakes

  • Landing late. The walk exists to lead the ear to beat 1. If the new chord comes late, drop to a single linking note.
  • Too many links. A walk into every chord gets busy. Save them for the ends of phrases and section changes.
  • Wrong direction. Walking up C–D–E–F–G ends on a G that is too high and takes too long. Choose the short road.
  • Notes ringing together. Lift the previous finger as you play the next note, so the walk sounds like a line, not a smear.
  • Losing the strum. Keep your strumming hand moving through the walk (miss the strings on the up-strokes) so you don't lose the beat.
Going further
  • Alternate bass chords like C/G (C with G in the bass) give you new starting points: from C/G the bass steps straight down G → F into an F barre chord.
  • Approach notes: a single note one fret below the target root (B → C, F# → G, G# → A) is the smallest possible link.
  • Learn the neck: every walk you work out teaches you note names on the bass strings. Experimenting is the point.
